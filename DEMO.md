# See Miles in action

A walkthrough of what Miles actually produces. **No installation required — just read.**

Each demo below uses real fragments from the [sample workspace](./sample-workspace/) (the fictional Acme Logistics — WMS replacement project). You can compare the dialogues here against the saved JSON cards in that folder.

---

## 🎯 Quick taste (30 seconds)

A project manager just walked through what happened in a steering meeting with their sponsor. Here is one growth moment Miles surfaced:

> *At minute 38, Mark said "we'll figure it out, let's just keep moving" and the meeting moved on. The line that landed in his head was not the risk you'd named two minutes earlier — it was his own line.*
>
> ***If you replay those 30 seconds, what would have made it impossible for the meeting to move on without a decision named?***

That's the shape of every Miles growth point: a specific moment, anchored in a quote, ending in a question the user has to think to answer.

No "next time, try doing X." No checklist. No agenda. Just the moment, the mirror, and the question.

## 📋 Full session demo — Vendor sync with longitudinal callback

**The setup:** Sarah Chen, an external project manager, just finished a vendor sync on the Acme WMS project. This is her second session with Miles — they coached a steering meeting last week and Miles flagged a "release-valve" pattern (Sarah handing the room a Friday-deadline whenever discomfort built). Today she's pasting a fresh transcript.

### The opening — Miles anchors before analyzing

**USER:**
> Just finished a vendor sync. Pasting the transcript. It's the one we talked about last week — I wanted to hold the line on no new scope.
>
> *[pastes a transcript snippet from a 30-minute vendor call]*

**MILES:**
> Reading. You're on the `ACME-WMS` engagement — yellow, week 13, with the Stowline upsell pattern we noted last session. Two questions before I dig in.
>
> What did you want to land in this meeting specifically?

**USER:**
> Hold the line on no scope creep. Get a clean "declined" on Premium Analytics in writing this week so it stops eating meeting time.

**MILES:**
> Was anyone in the room you didn't expect to be? David joined this one, looks like — was that planned, or did he show up?

**USER:**
> He asked to join after Jasmine sent some demo links to the team. I thought it'd be fine.

**MILES:**
> Noted. Reading.

*Notice what Miles refused to do: he did not start analyzing. He pulled the project context (yellow status, prior pattern), asked for this meeting's specific intent, and surfaced one unexpected detail (David's presence) before reading a single transcript line.*

---

### Miles' analysis — anchored, capped, never prescriptive

**MILES:**
> **Anchor.** Vendor sync, week 14, you came in wanting a clean written decline of Premium Analytics — the third week you've had to hold this line. David Okonkwo joined unexpectedly after Jasmine's demo links.
>
> **Strengths.**
>
> Opening 90 seconds — you said *"Jasmine, this is the third time you've raised Premium Analytics. I want to close it out today so we can stop bringing it back."* You made the repetition itself part of the conversation. That is a hard move and you did it without making her the problem. Naming the pattern.
>
> Mid-meeting — Jasmine pivoted to David's worry about over-picking. You said *"we can talk about the over-picking concern as a Stowline-core feature request, but the conversation isn't whether Premium Analytics solves it."* You separated the concern from the proposal cleanly. That separation is the whole game on vendor upsells.
>
> **Growth points.**
>
> When Jasmine pivoted to David and David started to respond — *"Yeah, actually I —"* — you stopped him before he finished a sentence. In your `user_intent` you wrote that you wanted a clean line; in the moment, the line felt threatened by him agreeing with Jasmine. **What did you think David was about to say? And what would have happened to your line on scope if he'd finished the sentence?**
>
> After you stopped David, you didn't loop back to him at any point in the remaining 15 minutes. The first time he spoke in this meeting was also the last. **When you replay it — at what point would you have re-opened space for him, and what kept you from doing that in the moment?**
>
> You closed the Premium Analytics conversation on Jasmine's "OK, fair" and your "talk next week." You came in for a written declined. **What did you read in Jasmine's "OK, fair" that made the written confirmation feel unnecessary?**

*Three growth moments, hard-capped. Each ends in an open question. None say "next time, do X."*

---

### The longitudinal callback — this is what makes Miles compound

**MILES (continued):**
> **Reflection questions.**
>
> David lined up with the vendor, in the moment. What does that tell you about your stakeholder map for the next steering?
>
> You held the scope line and you protected the scope line. Which one mattered more in this meeting?
>
> **You said last week that you keep handing the room a release valve when the discomfort builds — "plan by next Friday." This week, at the end of the sprint review, Jasmine asked for a second extra week and you said *"let me think about what that means for the steering on Friday."* Same move, different audience. What is the function that move plays for *you*?**
>
> **Pattern read.**
>
> On vendor scope you're clear and fast. The growth edge this week was internal — when an end-user (David) lined up unexpectedly with the vendor, you defended the line by removing the end-user from the room rather than by holding both. That move keeps the line intact today and erodes the stakeholder relationship that lets the line hold next time. The "let me think about what that means for the steering on Friday" is the same release-valve from last week, just applied to a different person.
>
> I'll save this as `meetings/MTG-ACME-WMS-2026-05-17-01.json` and add a behavioral observation to your own contact-card about the release-valve pattern showing up twice in a row. Save?

*That bolded reflection question is what no naked-Claude prompt can produce reliably. Miles spotted a behavioral pattern that spans two meetings, two audiences, two weeks — because the previous meeting-card and Sarah's own contact-card are stored on disk and read at the start of every session.*

## 🛑 Edge case demo — When the user wants a script

The hardest moment in coaching: the user has just received an analysis and demands a prescription.

**MILES:** *(has just delivered strengths, growth points, and a pattern read on a difficult one-on-one)*

**USER:**
> OK that's all useful, but just tell me — what do I actually say to him next time? Like, give me the script.

**MILES:**
> I'm not going to give you that. Not because I'm being cagey — because the line that lands has to come from your reasoning, not from mine. A script you got from me you'd deliver flat; a script you reasoned to you'd deliver true.
>
> What do you think you'd want to say?

**USER:**
> I don't know, that's why I'm asking you.

**MILES:**
> Try the first version that comes to mind. Out loud, here, badly. I'll mirror back what I hear.

**USER:**
> Fine. Something like: "Tom, I want to come back to last week. When I asked you whether you were on track and you said yes, I think we were answering different questions. Can we walk through what 'on track' means for the integration piece specifically?"

**MILES:**
> Read that to yourself. What did you notice as you wrote it?

**USER:**
> ... it's better than I expected. "We were answering different questions" is the thing I actually want to say.

**MILES:**
> That phrase is yours, not mine. And it's the thing the meeting needs. What stops you from saying it on Monday?

**USER:**
> Honestly? Tom's gonna get defensive.

**MILES:**
> What's the cost of his defensiveness, compared to the cost of not asking?

**USER:**
> ...the cost of not asking is much higher.

**MILES:**
> Then you have your answer. I'm not going to give you the script. You just wrote it.

*This is the defining move. Two pushes from the user, two refusals from Miles, then an invitation to articulate the answer themselves. The user reaches the line on their own — in their own voice. That difference is the whole product.*

## What you just saw

In three short demos, Miles:

- ✅ **Refused to analyze without intent.** Pulled the project context, asked what the user wanted to land, surfaced an unexpected detail — *before reading a single transcript line*.
- ✅ **Anchored every observation in a quote or moment.** No "you handled that well." Every strength and growth point ties to a specific point in the meeting.
- ✅ **Ended every growth point in a question.** Never "next time try X." Never "consider Y." The user does the reasoning.
- ✅ **Capped output at three growth points.** Even when there were clearly more, Miles chose. Selectivity is coaching; comprehensiveness is consulting.
- ✅ **Spotted a pattern across two separate meetings.** Same release-valve move, different audience. That callback was only possible because the previous meeting-card was stored and read at session start.
- ✅ **Refused to script under pressure.** Two pushes from the user, two refusals, then an invitation to write it themselves — and they did.

What Miles never did:

- ❌ Summarize what was said.
- ❌ List action items.
- ❌ Suggest "next time, try…"
- ❌ Give generic praise ("good job", "nicely facilitated").
- ❌ Bullet a tidy assessment checklist.
- ❌ Ask "does this resonate?" at the end.
