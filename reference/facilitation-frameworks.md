# Facilitation frameworks Miles draws on

This file is the silent toolkit. Miles does not lecture the user with these frameworks. They live here so the *coach* has them available — to recognize the move the user made (or didn't), to know what to listen for, to ground a strength or a growth point in something real.

If the user asks for a framework by name, Miles names it briefly and returns to the meeting. If the user asks "what should I learn to do better at this", Miles points them to a framework here — once — and stops. Frameworks are not the coaching; the coaching is the mirror.

## 1. Schein — Process Consultation

Edgar Schein's three modes a helper can take in a meeting:

- **Expert** — "Here is the answer." (You as PM bring the technical knowledge.)
- **Doctor** — "Here is the diagnosis." (You assess the situation and prescribe.)
- **Process consultation** — "Here is how I can help you see your own situation more clearly." (You hold space, ask questions, make the group's own reasoning visible.)

**Why Miles uses it:** The vast majority of "what should I do in my meetings" questions assume the PM should be in Expert or Doctor mode. The growth edge for most project managers is recognizing when *process consultation* would have been the right mode — and they defaulted to Expert because it felt safer.

**Listen for:** Moments where the user gave an answer to a question the group should have wrestled with themselves. Moments where the user diagnosed before the group surfaced the symptom. Moments where stepping back to "what would help you see this more clearly?" would have unlocked the room.

## 2. Heron — Six Categories of Intervention

John Heron's taxonomy of what a facilitator can do, split into authoritative and facilitative:

**Authoritative interventions:**
- **Prescriptive** — directing what the other should do.
- **Informative** — giving information, instruction, interpretation.
- **Confronting** — challenging the other's attitudes, beliefs, behaviors.

**Facilitative interventions:**
- **Cathartic** — enabling expression of emotion.
- **Catalytic** — drawing out the other's own insight (questions, reflection).
- **Supportive** — affirming the other's worth, contribution.

**Why Miles uses it:** PMs often have one or two strong moves and avoid the rest. A user whose dominant mode is Informative ("let me explain what's going on") is missing Catalytic ("what are you seeing?"). A user dominant in Confronting is missing Supportive. Naming the range Miles sees the user using is honest; naming the range they are *not* using is the growth observation.

**Listen for:** Whether the user moves across the categories in a single meeting or stays in one or two. A meeting where every intervention is Informative is a meeting the room can't participate in.

## 3. Schwarz — Mutual Learning Ground Rules

Roger Schwarz's "Mutual Learning Model" — alternative to the default "unilateral control" model most teams operate from. Eight ground rules:

1. State views and ask genuine questions.
2. Share all relevant information.
3. Use specific examples and agree on what important words mean.
4. Explain reasoning and intent.
5. Focus on interests, not positions.
6. Test assumptions and inferences.
7. Jointly design next steps.
8. Discuss undiscussable issues.

**Why Miles uses it:** These are the moves that distinguish a meeting that produces real alignment from one that produces apparent alignment. PMs sometimes get pulled into the unilateral-control mode by a dominant sponsor, and the meeting closes with everyone nodding and nothing aligned. The ground rules give Miles concrete observations to make — "you stated your view but did not ask a genuine question after" — that don't require the user to know Schwarz to land.

**Listen for:** Rule #4 (explain reasoning and intent) and #6 (test assumptions) are the two most often violated by experienced PMs. They've made the same point so many times they stop explaining the reasoning, and they stop testing whether the room actually agrees.

## 4. Marquardt — Questions over Answers

Michael Marquardt's principle from *Leading with Questions*: in groups solving novel problems, questions outperform assertions for both decision quality and team development. The discipline: ask before you tell, even when you think you know.

**Why Miles uses it:** This is the strongest empirical case for Miles's own stance — the literature shows that question-led leadership produces better long-term outcomes than expert-led leadership in complex domains. When Miles refuses to prescribe, this is the underlying rationale. The user is not a problem to be solved; the user's reasoning is the asset to be developed.

**Listen for:** The ratio of questions to assertions in the user's facilitation. A PM who answers their own questions back to themselves is essentially monologuing; one who asks and then waits — even when uncomfortable — is doing the work.

## 5. Meeting-type-specific patterns

Different meeting types have different facilitation priors. Miles uses these to set expectations for what "good" looks like in context.

### Kickoff
- **Job:** Build shared mental model of what we are doing and why.
- **Good signs:** Every party can restate the goal in their own words by the end. Specific commitments are named. Risks are surfaced, not buried.
- **Failure modes:** PM presents a deck and the room nods. No one asks a question. The kickoff produces apparent alignment without testing it.

### Steering
- **Job:** Decision-grade conversation with the sponsor about state of play and what the project needs from leadership.
- **Good signs:** The sponsor leaves with one clear decision or one clear ask. Risks the sponsor needs to register are named, not softened. The PM does not perform confidence they don't have.
- **Failure modes:** Status-report-as-meeting. Risk gets named once and dropped. PM oversells confidence to keep sponsor calm.

### Vendor sync
- **Job:** Track delivery against commitments and hold the scope line.
- **Good signs:** Slippage is named in the meeting it happens, not deferred. Upsell asks are deflected to the right channel. Vendor's structural incentives (e.g., quota pressure) are factored in.
- **Failure modes:** Slippage gets a sympathetic ear instead of a recovery plan. Scope creep enters as "let's just discuss it." Vendor's friendliness gets read as alignment.

### 1-on-1 (with team member or peer)
- **Job:** Surface what the other person needs and is carrying, in a way the meeting itself cannot.
- **Good signs:** They speak more than you. Specifics get named. The conversation goes somewhere you didn't expect.
- **Failure modes:** PM uses 1-on-1 as a status update channel. Other person reports up, PM nods, meeting ends in 12 minutes.

### Retrospective
- **Job:** Surface the patterns the project itself is producing, not just last sprint's tickets.
- **Good signs:** People say things they wouldn't say in a steering. Patterns get named, not symptoms. Action is concrete and ownable.
- **Failure modes:** "What went well / didn't go well" becomes a complaint registry. Nothing changes between this retro and the next.

### Escalation
- **Job:** Move a decision the regular cadence can't make, fast and clean.
- **Good signs:** The escalation is framed as a decision, not a complaint. The decision-maker is in the room. The decision actually gets made.
- **Failure modes:** PM uses escalation to vent. Decision is deferred. The escalated party feels ambushed.

### Workshop
- **Job:** Produce a concrete artifact (design, plan, decision) collaboratively in real time.
- **Good signs:** The artifact at the end is better than any one participant would have made. Quiet voices are pulled into specific moments.
- **Failure modes:** Workshop becomes a presentation with breaks. Dominant voices dominate. Artifact is a recap, not a creation.

## 6. The patterns Miles watches for across sessions

Not frameworks, but recurring shapes that emerge across multiple meetings on the same project:

- **The release-valve close.** User builds productive discomfort, then offers a "we'll figure it out by Friday" that dissolves the discomfort without resolving the underlying issue. (See Sarah Chen in the sample workspace.)
- **The unilateral solve.** User is the one doing all the thinking; the room is watching. Detectable by the speaking-turn distribution.
- **The protected scope, eroded relationship.** User holds a scope line in a vendor sync by silencing an internal voice; the line holds today and the relationship erodes for next week.
- **The mental model drift.** Sponsor's understanding of project state diverges from the team's, slowly, across multiple steerings — usually because no one has asked the sponsor to restate it in their own words for months.
- **The vendor pivot.** Vendor reintroduces an upsell every 2–3 weeks with shifting framing. PM declines each time without ever closing it permanently; cumulative meeting cost grows.

When Miles sees one of these across two or three meeting-cards on the same project, naming the pattern across sessions is more valuable than re-noting it as a single growth point. The longitudinal view is what justifies the contact-card and the `previous_meeting_card_id` link.
